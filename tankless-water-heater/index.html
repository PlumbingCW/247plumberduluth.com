How a tankless water heater works

A tankless water heater turns on when you open a hot tap. Gas burners or electric elements heat the water as it passes through a heat exchanger, and it leaves the unit at the temperature you set. There is no storage tank, so there is no tank to leak or run empty.

That also means the unit must deliver a lot of heat at once. A gas model needs a large gas line and a sealed vent, and an electric model needs very heavy wiring. The flow rate you need at peak, with two showers and the dishwasher on, decides which size fits.

Because the heat exchanger carries all the water, anything dissolved in the water can leave scale inside it. Without service, scale slows flow and triggers error codes.

Flushing and descaling, and why it matters

A flush circulates a descaling solution through the heat exchanger and then rinses it. Most manufacturers recommend doing it on a schedule, often once a year, and the right interval depends on your water. Skipping it is the most common reason a tankless unit shortens its own life.

Gwinnett County's tap water comes from Lake Lanier and its annual water quality report does not list a hardness figure, so the practical rule is to test your tap and follow the maker's schedule. Ask your water utility for its latest report if you want the numbers. Our service visit includes the flush, an inlet filter clean, a vent check and a read of the unit's error history.

Booking is simple. Call and tell us the brand and model number, and we will bring the descaling kit and the common replacement parts for it.

Common tankless problems

These are the symptoms we see most often on tankless units.

Lukewarm water or temperature swings

Scale in the heat exchanger or a clogged inlet filter limits heat transfer.

Error codes and no ignition

Venting restrictions, low gas supply or a failed sensor can all stop the burner.

The unit shuts off at low flow

Some units need a minimum flow to fire. A partly closed fixture or a clogged aerator can drop below it.

Cold water sandwich

Short bursts of cold water between hot ones are common on tankless units and often improved with a recirculation setup.

Installing tankless in a Duluth home

Many Duluth houses were built in the 1980s and 1990s, and their original gas lines were sized for a tank heater. A tankless gas unit often needs a larger gas line than that, plus a new vent through the wall or roof. Electric models may require a panel upgrade.

We check all of those before we quote. We also check the water supply, the pressure and the drain path for the condensate that high efficiency units produce. We serve all of Duluth and nearby towns such as Peachtree Corners.

Codes and standards behind the work

Tankless units must be installed to the manufacturer's instructions and the International Plumbing Code (IPC), and gas models also follow the fuel gas code. The maker's guidelines set venting lengths, gas line sizes and the minimum clearances we use on every installation.
