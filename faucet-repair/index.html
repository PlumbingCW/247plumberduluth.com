Why faucets drip and leak

Most faucets fail in a predictable way. A rubber washer or seal wears out, a ceramic cartridge cracks or a valve seat corrodes. Minerals in the water add to the wear, and the faucet starts to drip, spit or leak around the handle.

A slow drip may seem harmless, but it adds up. The Environmental Protection Agency's WaterSense program notes that a faucet dripping once per second wastes a surprising amount of water over a year, and it also stains the basin and wears the finish.

Leaks around the base or under the sink matter more. Water that gets into a cabinet damages wood and breeds mold. If you see dampness under the sink, turn off the shutoff valves and call us.

These are the faucet repairs we do most often.

Kitchen, bath and outdoor faucets

Kitchen faucets get the most use and often have a pull-down sprayer that can leak at the hose. Bathroom faucets are simpler, but their drains and pop-up stoppers often fail, too. We handle both, along with laundry sinks and utility sinks.

Outdoor hose bibs matter in this area. A hose bib that drips or has a cracked body after a freeze can leak inside the wall. We repair or replace them, and we can install a frost-proof model. If a faucet leak is part of a bigger water problem, see leak detection.

Faucet repair in Duluth homes

Many Duluth houses built in the 1980s and 1990s still have original faucets and shutoff valves under the sinks. Those valves often seize or weep when turned, so we replace them during the visit to make the next repair easy.

Small habits that keep faucets working

Clean the aerator every few months. Unscrew the tip, rinse out the grit and soak it in white vinegar if it has mineral buildup. This simple step restores flow in many low-pressure complaints and costs nothing.

Do not overtighten handles. Forcing a handle shut wears out the washer or the cartridge quickly and is behind many drips. If a handle needs force to stop the water, the faucet needs service, not more muscle.

Know where the shutoff valves are. Every sink has two small valves under it, and turning them clockwise stops the water to that sink only. Test them once a year so they do not freeze up before the day you need them.

Codes and standards behind the work

Federal rules limit faucet flow, and the EPA's WaterSense program labels bathroom faucets that use 1.5 gallons per minute or less. Fixtures should also meet the standards in the International Plumbing Code (IPC). We keep these in mind when we recommend a replacement.
