Tankless Water Heater Heat Exchanger: A Duluth, GA Guide

The heat exchanger is the most expensive part of a tankless water heater and the one most often hurt by neglect. Here is how it works, how it fails and how to keep it healthy.

What the heat exchanger does

A tankless water heater has no tank to hold hot water. Instead, water passes through a coil or a set of narrow channels called the heat exchanger. A gas burner or an electric element heats the metal, and the metal heats the water as it flows through.

That design is efficient because heat is made only on demand. It also means the narrow passages inside the heat exchanger have to stay clear. Anything that coats the inside, such as mineral scale, reduces how well heat moves into the water and forces the unit to work harder.

Some units have a primary heat exchanger and a second one that captures extra heat from the exhaust. The second one in condensing models is made of stainless steel to handle acidic condensate.

How a heat exchanger fails

Failures are rarely sudden. They develop over years, and the symptoms are easy to spot if you know them.

Scale buildup

Dissolved minerals harden inside the passages. Flow drops, the temperature swings and error codes appear.

Corrosion

Acidic condensate or poor venting can corrode a heat exchanger from the outside.

Overheating

Low flow and scale can cause hot spots that crack the metal.

Freeze damage

A unit in an unheated space can freeze and split in a hard cold snap.

Leaks

Pinholes in the exchanger show up as water inside the cabinet or on the floor below.

Clogged inlet filter

A blocked screen starves the unit and imitates a heat exchanger problem.

Signs to watch for

If you notice these, schedule service before the damage spreads.

  • 1Hot water turns lukewarm or swings between hot and cold during a shower.
  • 2The unit shows an error code about overheating, flow or temperature, and the code returns after a reset.
  • 3Water takes much longer to heat or flow seems weaker than before.
  • 4The unit makes boiling, kettle-like or rumbling noises as it runs.
  • 5Water appears under or inside the unit's cabinet.

How flushing protects the heat exchanger

A descaling flush circulates a mild acid, usually white vinegar or a manufacturer-approved solution, through the heat exchanger and then rinses it out. It dissolves scale before it can harden into a blockage. Most makers recommend a flush on a regular schedule, often once a year.

Cleaning the inlet filter at the same visit keeps debris out of the passages. A flush needs isolation valves on the hot and cold lines, and a unit installed without them is harder to service. We add the valves when we find a unit without them.

Ask us about the full service on the tankless water heater page. For a unit that is past saving, water heater replacement covers the options.

What a service visit looks at

A tankless service visit starts with the error history stored in the unit. The log shows which codes have appeared and how often, and it tells the technician where to look. Next come the inlet filter, the water flow and the temperature rise across the unit.

The technician then flushes the heat exchanger, checks the burner or the elements, inspects the vent and tests the gas pressure on a gas model. Findings are explained in plain language, with photos if something looks wrong.

Water treatment and your heat exchanger

Water that leaves heavy scale can be treated at the source. A whole-house scale control device or a softener lowers what reaches the heat exchanger, and maker instructions often say what water quality is acceptable for warranty coverage.

If you are unsure about your water, start with your utility's report or a simple test kit. We can talk you through the options when we service the unit.

Questions to ask before you pay for a new heat exchanger

A good technician will answer these without hesitation.

  • 1What failed, and can you show me?

  • 3What caused the failure, and will it happen again?
  • 4What is the price of the repair compared with a new unit?

Tankless heat exchangers in Duluth

Duluth's drinking water comes from Gwinnett County Water Resources, which draws on Lake Lanier and the Chattahoochee River and publishes an annual water quality report. Because that report does not give a figure we can quote for scale risk, the safe approach is to follow the maker's flush interval and to test your tap or ask the utility for its latest numbers.

Many Duluth tankless units sit in garages and exterior closets. Georgia gets hard freezes some winters, so a unit in an unheated space needs freeze protection that follows the manufacturer's instructions. We service tankless units across the city and in nearby Peachtree Corners.

Maker guidelines and the International Plumbing Code (IPC) both treat the heat exchanger as a service item, so follow the flush schedule in your manual.

Repair or replace a damaged heat exchanger

A new heat exchanger is among the costliest parts of a tankless unit, so the repair is judged against the age and condition of the whole heater. A unit that is only a few years old and still under warranty is usually worth fixing. A unit that is more than ten years old with other worn parts may be cheaper to replace.

Ask for a written explanation of the damage and a price for both options. We will show you what we saw and let you decide without pressure.
