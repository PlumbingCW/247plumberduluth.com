Water Heater Relief Valve Dripping: A Duluth, GA Guide

The temperature and pressure relief valve is a safety device, and a drip from its pipe is a warning. This guide explains what a drip means, what is safe to check and what should never be done.

What the relief valve is for

The temperature and pressure relief valve, often called the T&P valve, sits on the side or the top of the tank. If the temperature or pressure inside gets too high, the valve opens and releases water to prevent the tank from failing violently. A discharge pipe carries that water to a safe spot near the floor.

Because it is a safety device, a leak from it should not be ignored. The cause may be harmless, such as thermal expansion, or a sign of a problem that needs attention fast.

Why the valve drips

These are the common causes.

High water pressure

Pressure above the safe range from the supply pushes the valve open.

Thermal expansion

In a closed system, heated water expands and raises pressure. An expansion tank absorbs it.

Thermostat set too high

Water hotter than the setting can open the valve by temperature.

Failed or worn valve

Mineral buildup keeps the seat from sealing, and the valve drips.

Sediment in the tank

Sediment can cause hot spots that raise the temperature.

A failing tank

A tank at the end of its life may overheat or leak and the valve reacts.

Safe checks

These steps are for observation only.

  • 1Look at the discharge pipe. Note whether it drips, runs or is steady, and whether the water is hot.
  • 2Check the thermostat. Make sure it is set at or below 120 degrees Fahrenheit, the level the Department of Energy suggests for most homes.
  • 3Check the water pressure. A gauge on a hose bib shows the pressure. A reading above about 80 psi is too high.
  • 4Look for an expansion tank. A small tank on the cold line near the heater absorbs expansion in a closed system.
  • 5Look for rust. Rust at the valve or the tank base points to corrosion.
  • 6Call for help. If the valve drips steadily, or the tank is hot and rumbling, call right away.

What never to do

Never plug, cap or block the valve or its discharge pipe. Never replace the discharge pipe with a smaller one or one that points up. The valve must be free to open, or the tank can build dangerous pressure.

If the valve is discharging forcefully, or the tank is hissing, shake or hot, shut off the gas or power and leave the area, then call us at once. For the repair itself see water heater repair, and for a new tank see water heater replacement.

What a technician tests

We start with the pressure at a hose bib and the water temperature at the tap and the tank. Then we check for an expansion tank and test the relief valve by lifting the lever briefly with a bucket under the discharge. A valve that does not reseat cleanly after the test is replaced.

If the pressure is above the safe range, we install or adjust a pressure reducing valve. If the system is closed by a check valve or a reducing valve, we add or recharge an expansion tank.

Why the discharge pipe matters

The discharge pipe carries water from the valve to a safe place, usually within a few inches of the floor or to a drain. It must be as large as the valve outlet and must not have a valve, a cap or an upward turn. A pipe that is blocked will not let the valve work.

In attics, the discharge pipe should lead to a drain pan or an outside point. A leaking valve above a finished ceiling can cause expensive damage before anyone notices.

When replacement is the better answer

If the tank is old, rusted or leaking from the body, a new valve will not solve the cause. A tank over ten or twelve years old that is dripping at the valve and showing rust at the bottom is a candidate for replacement.

Document the problem for the technician

Note when the dripping started, whether it happens after showers or all day and what the water pressure reads. Take a photo of the discharge pipe and the tank base. These details help the technician decide quickly whether the cause is pressure, temperature or a worn valve.

A simple yearly relief valve check

Once a year, place a bucket under the discharge pipe and lift the valve lever briefly. Water should flow and then stop when you release it. If it keeps dripping, the seat may be worn and the valve needs replacing. If the lever will not move, do not force it, and call a plumber to look at the valve.

What to do right away if the valve is discharging

These steps keep you safe while you wait.

  • 1Turn off the gas or the electric breaker to the heater.
  • 2Keep everyone away from the discharge pipe and the area around the tank.
  • 3Do not touch the valve or the pipe, which may be very hot.
  • 4Shut off the cold water supply to the tank if you can reach it safely.
  • 5Call us and describe what you see, hear and smell.

Pressure and relief valves in Duluth

Gwinnett County Water Resources delivers water to Duluth homes at pressures that vary with the neighborhood and the elevation. A home on a low point may see higher pressure at the house, and a pressure reducing valve protects the plumbing. Ask us to read the pressure at your hose bib.

Georgia plumbing code addresses relief valves and expansion control, and permit rules vary, so ask the City of Duluth permits and inspections office if the work needs a permit. We serve Duluth and nearby Peachtree Corners.

The International Plumbing Code (IPC) and ANSI Z21.22 set the requirements for relief valves, which is why a failed valve should be replaced with an approved part.
