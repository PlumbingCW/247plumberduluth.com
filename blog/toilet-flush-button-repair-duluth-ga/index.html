Toilet Flush Button Repair for Duluth, GA Homes

A flush button that sticks, sags or does nothing is usually a simple mechanical fault. This guide shows the common causes, a safe do-it-yourself fix and the signs that the problem is bigger.

How a flush button works

A push-button toilet looks different from a handle toilet, but the idea is the same. Pressing the button pulls on a rod or a cable, which lifts a flapper or a canister valve inside the tank. Water then drops into the bowl and flushes the waste.

Dual-flush buttons have two sections, a small one for liquids and a large one for solids. Each moves a different amount of rod and releases a different amount of water. Because the button sits in the tank lid, it takes constant pressing, and the plastic parts wear.

Most flush button problems come from the linkage under the lid, not the button itself.

Common flush button problems

These are the faults we see most.

Button stuck down

A bent rod or a worn guide keeps the button from springing back, and the toilet runs.

Button does nothing

The rod has slipped off the flapper chain or lever, or the chain is too slack.

Weak flush

The chain is too long or too short, or mineral buildup restricts the water.

Button wobbles or clicks

The mounting nut is loose or the plastic has cracked.

Only one half works

One of the two linkages has come loose or broken.

Constant running

The flapper is not sealing because of a tight chain or a stuck button.

A simple repair in six steps

Most fixes need no tools beyond pliers.

  • 1Turn off the water. Close the shutoff valve behind the toilet and flush to empty the tank.
  • 2Lift the lid carefully. Set the tank lid on a towel. It is porcelain and heavy, so avoid dropping it.
  • 3Check the linkage. Look at the rods and the chain connected to the flapper. Reconnect any loose parts.
  • 4Adjust the chain. Leave only a little slack. A chain that is too short keeps the flapper open, and one that is too long fails to lift it.
  • 5Tighten or replace the button. Hand-tighten the mounting nut. If the button is cracked, replace the assembly, which is sold as a kit.
  • 6Test several flushes. Turn on the water, let the tank fill and flush a few times to confirm the repair.

When it is not just the button

A toilet that runs after the button is fixed probably has a worn flapper or a fill valve problem. A toilet that flushes weakly may have a partial clog, which is covered in our toilet repair page. Water around the base points to a failed wax ring.

Older toilets may no longer have parts, and a dual-flush kit may not fit a tank from an older model. If a toilet needs several repairs, a newer one can cost less than another visit, and it also uses less water.

Choosing a replacement button kit

Flush button kits are sold by the size of the opening in the tank lid and the length of the rod. Measure the hole and the distance from the lid to the flapper before you buy. A kit that is a half inch too long will not close the flapper, and one that is too short will not lift it.

Some toilets use a button that is part of the tank lid, which you must replace as one unit. If the lid is cracked or the model is old, a new toilet may cost less than hunting for parts.

When a plumber visit makes sense

If you have fixed the button and the toilet still behaves oddly, the problem is probably below the lid. A fill valve that sticks, a worn flapper seat and mineral buildup in the flush passages all need tools and a trained eye.

A toilet that rocks or leaks at the base should be looked at at once, because the seal under it has failed and water may be reaching the floor.

Mistakes that make the problem worse

These errors turn a quick fix into a bigger repair.

  • 1Forcing a stuck button, which snaps the rod or cracks the lid.
  • 2Setting the chain too tight, which leaves the flapper open and the toilet running.
  • 3Using harsh tank cleaning tablets that rot flappers and seals.
  • 4Lifting the tank lid by the button, which tears the linkage.
  • 5Ignoring a toilet that runs, which wastes water every hour.

Flush button repairs and water use in Duluth

Gwinnett County Water Resources runs a water conservation program and encourages efficient fixtures. A toilet that sticks or runs wastes treated water that you are charged for, so a quick fix helps your bill.

Duluth homes built in the 1980s and 1990s often have original toilets that use more water per flush than modern models. If you are replacing yours, look for a WaterSense label and ask us about installation. We serve all of Duluth and nearby Peachtree Corners.

The EPA's WaterSense label marks toilets at 1.28 gallons per flush or less, which is a useful guideline when a repair is not worth it.

How to prevent flush button trouble

Press the button firmly but not hard. Slamming the button breaks the rods over time. Clean the lid area with a soft cloth, because mineral deposits and cleaning products can gum up the guide.

Check inside the tank once a year. Look for a worn flapper, a loose chain and rust on the linkage, and replace parts that look tired. A few dollars of parts can save a service call later.
