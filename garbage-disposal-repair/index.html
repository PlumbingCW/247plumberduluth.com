Disposal problems and what they mean

A disposal that hums and does not spin is jammed. A disposal that does nothing at all has tripped its reset button or lost power. One that leaks has a failed seal, a loose connection or a cracked housing.

Never put your hand into a disposal, even when it is off. Turn off the switch and unplug the unit or switch off the breaker before checking anything. The hex slot at the bottom of most units lets you turn the blades by hand with a wrench to free a jam.

If the sink backs up when the disposal runs, the clog may be in the drain beyond the unit, and cleaning the line is the fix.

What we repair

These are the disposal repairs we do most.

Jammed blades

We free the flywheel, remove the object and test the unit.

Tripped or dead unit

Leaks at the sink flange

Leaks at the housing

A cracked housing cannot be repaired and the disposal is replaced.

Drain and dishwasher connection

Loose hose clamps and clogged dishwasher ports are common sources of leaks.

Bad smells

Buildup in the chamber and baffle is cleaned out.

Keep a disposal working longer

A few habits prevent most jams and clogs.

  • 1Run cold water before, during and for 15 seconds after grinding.
  • 2Feed small amounts of food at a time rather than a full load.
  • 3Keep grease, coffee grounds, eggshells in bulk, bones, fibrous peels and pasta or rice out of the unit.
  • 4Never pour grease down the sink. Gwinnett County asks households to keep fats, oils and grease out of drains.
  • 5Clean the chamber with ice and a little citrus peel for odor, not harsh chemicals.

Repair or replace

A jam or a reset is a repair. A cracked housing, a seized motor or a unit older than about a decade is usually a replacement, because parts cost nearly as much as a new unit.

When we replace a disposal, we match horsepower to your use, install a new mounting assembly, reconnect the dishwasher drain, and test for leaks. If the sink drain backs up, drain cleaning clears the line.

Disposals in Duluth homes

Kitchens in homes from the 1980s and 1990s often have disposals that are well past their service life, plus P-traps and drain lines that have collected years of grease. We replace worn parts as we go and warn you when the line itself needs service.

What happens during a repair visit

We start by shutting off power at the switch and the breaker. Then we look under the sink at the mounting, the drain connections and the dishwasher hose, because most leaks start at one of those joints rather than inside the unit.

Next we free the blades, run the unit with water and watch for leaks and strange noises. If the unit works, we tell you what caused the problem so it does not return. If the motor or the housing is failing, we show you why and quote a replacement.

We leave the cabinet clean and the sink draining freely. If the drain line past the disposal needs cleaning, we say so and quote that separately.

Codes and standards behind the work

Disposals connect to the sink trap, so the International Plumbing Code (IPC) rules for traps and drain connections apply. According to Gwinnett County guidelines, grease should not go down any drain, and a disposal does not change that rule.
