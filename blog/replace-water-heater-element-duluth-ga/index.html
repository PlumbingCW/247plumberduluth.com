Replacing a Water Heater Element in Duluth, GA

Lukewarm water from an electric tank often means a failed heating element. This guide shows how to confirm the cause, what the replacement involves and when the job is better left to a plumber.

Signs the heating element has failed

An electric tank water heater has two elements, one near the top and one near the bottom. Each is a metal rod that heats the water around it. When one burns out, the tank still produces some hot water, which is why the problem often shows up as lukewarm showers or hot water that runs out fast.

Common signs include water that never gets fully hot, a tank that empties after a short shower, a breaker that trips when the heater runs and a rumbling noise as scale burns on the element. If you get no hot water at all, both elements may be out, or the problem may be the thermostat, the high limit switch or the power supply.

Testing comes before replacing. A multimeter tells you whether an element still has continuity, and a quick check of the breaker and reset button rules out the simple causes.

Testing and replacing an element, step by step

This is the general process for a standard tank. Always follow the label on your own heater.

  • 1Cut the power. Switch off the breaker for the water heater and confirm with a tester that the heater is dead. Never work on a live unit.
  • 2Shut off the water and drain some. Close the cold inlet valve and drain the tank below the level of the element, using a hose to a floor drain or the yard.
  • 3Open the access panel. Remove the cover and the insulation, then the plastic safety shield over the thermostat and element terminals.
  • 4Test the element. Disconnect the wires and check resistance across the terminals. A reading near zero or infinite signals a failed element.
  • 5Unscrew and replace it. Use an element wrench to remove the old one, clean the threads, fit a new gasket and tighten the new element to the correct wattage.
  • 6Refill before powering up. Open a hot tap to let air out while the tank fills. Only then restore power, because a dry element burns out in seconds.

When to call instead of doing it yourself

This is a task many handy homeowners can do, but it involves 240-volt power and a full tank. If you are not comfortable testing wiring or the tank is old, the safer move is to have a plumber do it. A tank that is more than ten years old and has rust at the base may not be worth repairing at all.

If you suspect the tank has failed instead, see our guide to water heater repair. When the tank is leaking from the body, water heater replacement is the answer, and no element will fix that.

Safety points that matter most

Water heater elements run on 240 volts, which is enough to cause a serious shock. Switch off the breaker, then test the wires at the terminals with a non-contact tester before you touch anything. Do not trust the thermostat setting or the label on the breaker panel alone.

Never turn the breaker back on until the tank is full. An element that runs dry overheats in seconds and burns out, and the new part is wasted. Open a hot tap upstairs, let the water run steadily until air stops sputtering, and only then restore power.

What a replacement costs and what else to check

An element is an inexpensive part, and the labor is usually under an hour. The bigger question is the rest of the tank. If you have opened the access panel, look at the insulation, the wiring and the thermostat for scorch marks or loose connections, and look at the drain valve for weeping.

If the tank is within a few years of its expected life, a new element buys time but may not be the best use of money. We tell you plainly what condition the tank is in, so you can choose between a repair and a new unit.

Ask also about the anode rod. It is a sacrificial rod that corrodes so the tank does not, and an old one that is eaten away leaves the steel exposed.

Mistakes that ruin a new element

Most repeat failures come from a short list of avoidable errors.

  • 1Powering the heater before the tank is full, which burns the new element at once.
  • 2Installing a wattage that does not match the heater label.
  • 3Reusing the old gasket, which can leak after tightening.
  • 4Over-tightening the element and cracking the threads in the tank.
  • 5Leaving the insulation and the safety cover off, which exposes wiring and wastes heat.

Water heater elements in Duluth

Gwinnett County Department of Water Resources supplies drinking water to Duluth, and the county publishes an annual water quality report. The report does not give us a hardness figure to quote here, so the practical step is to test your own tap or ask the utility. Minerals build up as scale on elements in any area, and flushing the tank each year slows it.

Many Duluth homes were built in the 1980s and 1990s, and some still have their second or third electric tank. If your tank has outlived its warranty, ask us to check the anode rod and the tank bottom before you buy parts. We serve the whole city and nearby Peachtree Corners.

The International Plumbing Code (IPC), which Georgia adopts with state amendments, requires a working relief valve on every tank, so check that too while the access panel is open.

Choosing the right element

Elements come in different wattages and styles. A low-density element has a longer life and is less prone to scale than a high-density one, and you must match the wattage printed on the old element or the heater label. Putting in a higher wattage than the tank allows is a fire risk.

Screw-in and bolt-in designs are not interchangeable, so bring the old element to the store or give the model number. Replace both elements if the tank is older, since the second one is usually near the end of its life, and you will have the tank open already.
